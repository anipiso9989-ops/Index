A random thought on note-taking, spun out from a backlog of other ideas:

If I bought a mini pocket notebook and forced myself to take all my notes inside it, a specific chain of events would trigger:

**Limited space → Forced compression → Radical prioritization → Abstraction**

The goal here isn't maximum information density; it’s maximum reconstructible understanding per unit of space.

I’d divide the notebook into units on the fly as I learn, rather than pre-allocating pages. At the end of each unit, I’d sketch a minimal mindmap—basically ([[minimapping]] stripped down to pure abstraction). The nodes would hold concept names, never full explanations.

- **The Notes:** Hold the actual knowledge.
- **The Mindmap:** Holds the conceptual skeleton.

This acts as a built-in retrieval test: looking at the skeleton should force you to mentally reconstruct the entire chapter.

The system operates across two deliberate levels of abstraction:
- **Local (Concept Schema):** Meaning → Representation → Context → Application → Boundaries
- **Global (Chapter Mindmap):** Concepts → Relationships → Hierarchy → Dependencies

Simply put: **Concept notes** handle understanding one thing; **Mindmaps** handle understanding the system of things.

### Execution Rules
- **One page = one idea.**
- Keep prose absolute bare-minimum.
- Prioritize models, relationships, formulas, core examples, and counter-intuitive insights over fluff.
- Don't force the schema if the material genuinely doesn't fit.

### The Schema: Quantitative Notes

I dug up an old note-taking schema I developed a while back (honestly thought I’d thrown it out). It’s heavily optimized for quantitative subjects, but adapts well to general learning:

1. **Concept (What is it?):** A precise definition and purpose. Concise, but sufficient to reconstruct the core idea.
2. **Code (How is it represented?):** Essential formulas, equations, notation, or rules. Only include alternate forms if they add distinct utility.
3. **Context (What does it connect to?):** Prerequisites, adjacent concepts, key variables, units, and structural relationships.
4. **Case (How is it used?):** One representative problem or example solved in minimal steps. Focus on high-value reasoning over mechanical algebra.
5. **Clarity (What gets misunderstood?):** Constraints, exceptions, edge cases, common pitfalls, and sharp insights.

### The Experiment: Multivariable Calculus

I’m running Multivariable Calculus through this as a testbed.

I'll evaluate the system across a few dimensions:
- **Compression:** Can an entire course cleanly fit into a single pocket notebook?
- **Reconstruction:** Can I fully recover a concept purely from these hyper-dense notes?
- **Learning & Problem Solving:** Does forcing this constraint actually improve understanding and raw mathematical ability?
- **Retrieval:** Can I rebuild a full chapter in my head just looking at the mindmap?
- **Flexibility & Retention:** Where does the schema break, and do these notes hold value weeks or months later?

**The Governing Metric:** How much usable capability does the notebook build—not how pretty the pages look.

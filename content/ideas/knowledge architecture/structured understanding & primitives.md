# Structured Understanding and Primitives

To solve novel problems, an intelligent system must decompose the problem space and construct a structured representation of it.

### The 10 Representational Categories
An intelligent system must represent the following aspects of a target system to achieve comprehension:

1. **Components:** What entities exist within the system?
2. **Properties:** What are the states and attributes of those entities?
3. **Relationships:** How are the entities spatially or logically related?
4. **Interactions:** How do the entities affect one another causally?
5. **Dynamics:** What changes occur over time?
6. **Constraints:** What boundaries or conditions limit the system?
7. **Function:** What behavior does the system exhibit?
8. **Context:** What larger hierarchy or environment is the system part of?
9. **Causal Origin:** Why does the system exist (how was it created)?
10. **Purpose:** What is the system's intended role (if a teleological context exists)?

### Reduction to Core Primitives
These ten categories can be compressed into three irreducible primitives:

$$ \text{Components} + \text{Relationships/Interactions} + \text{Transformations} $$

Under this framework, the remaining categories are derived:
- **Properties** = States of components.
- **Behavior** = Transformations over time.
- **Function** = Behavior interpreted relative to a goal or context.
- **Structure** = Components + relationships.
- **Causality** = Transformations under specific conditions.
- **Constraints** = Restrictions on possible transformations.
- **Purpose** = An internal or external goal explaining why a function is selected.

### The Problem of Hierarchical Components (The Whirlpool Problem)
Components are not necessarily fundamental physical objects. For example, a whirlpool is not a discrete physical object but a pattern of relationships and transformations among water molecules. 

However, a whirlpool can be treated as a single "component" at a macroscopic level of analysis, while water molecules are components at a microscopic level. Therefore, **components are hierarchical and scale-dependent**.

### Definition of Understanding
To understand a system $X$, an intelligent agent must represent:

1. Its constituents ($C$)
2. Their internal and external interactions ($I$)
3. How those interactions transform $X$ over time ($T$)

$$ U(X) = C(X) + I(X) + T(X) $$

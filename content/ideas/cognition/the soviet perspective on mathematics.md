The Soviets had a unique view on mathematics. 

They thought it to be the principal means by which a student could accelerate their mental development, and so they developed a culture centered around it. 

To them, mathematics was a way of thinking, not just a toolset; they used it to develop exceptionally high logical/quantitative mental power. What this means is that they didn't necessarily go into advanced math; Soviet mathematics gave you simple pre-requisites, but required brutally high reasoning. Problems were supposed to be difficult to reason through, despite being at a modest level of mathematical rigor (i.e. algebra, geometry, simple proofs).

The goal was to be able to develop that logical reasoning ability, and so they built a pipeline around it: scout kids from ordinary schools for talent, put them into specialized mathematics schools and get them to participate in Olympiads, and eventually let them go to elite universities where they can do mathematics research or teach the next generation.

This is the context of the situation in [[the theory of generalized learning development]]. 

The idea here that is interesting though, is their principle:
> Mathematical ability is developed by training mathematical reasoning, not merely learning more and more difficult subjects. 

The distinction between **advanced knowledge** and **advanced thought** is what's important here.
